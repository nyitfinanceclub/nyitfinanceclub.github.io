Cover Story · September 2026

The Month Interest Rates Came Back

A hot jobs report, stubborn inflation, and surging oil pushed the Fed into its first rate hike in three years, and sent bond yields to levels last seen before the 2008 financial crisis.

September opened with a surprise. The August jobs report, released on September 4, showed employers added 162,000 jobs, roughly three times what economists expected, while unemployment held at 4.1%. A week later, the August CPI report showed consumer prices up 3.4% from a year earlier, with gasoline alone driving more than a third of the monthly increase.

That combination left the Federal Reserve little room. On September 16, the FOMC voted 12–0 to raise the federal funds rate by a quarter point to 3.75–4.00%, its first increase since July 2023. Chair Kevin Warsh said inflation has been too high for too long, and 16 of 18 officials now project at least one more hike before the year ends.

The U.S. wasn't alone. The European Central Bank raised rates on September 10 and the Bank of Japan followed on September 18, lifting its rate to 1.25%, the highest since 1995. Meanwhile, the 10-year Treasury yield climbed past 5% and kept going, pushing 30-year mortgage rates to about 7.45%.

For students, the takeaway is simple: borrowing just got more expensive, saving just got more rewarding, and "higher for longer" is back as the phrase every finance interviewer will want you to explain.

Fed Hikes 25bps, Its First Increase Since July 2023

A unanimous vote, a hawkish dot plot, and a clear message from Chair Warsh: the inflation fight isn't over.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  1st October 2026

On September 16, the Federal Open Market Committee voted 12–0 to raise the federal funds rate by a quarter point, from 3.50–3.75% to 3.75–4.00%. It was the Fed's first hike since July 2023, and markets saw it coming: futures had priced better than a 90% chance of a move.

How we got here

The Fed held rates steady at every meeting from January through July. But in July, three officials (Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari, and Lorie Logan) had already dissented in favor of a hike. Then came a much stronger August jobs report and a CPI report showing inflation stuck at 3.4%, with energy prices doing much of the damage.

What Warsh said

Chair Kevin Warsh told reporters that inflation is too high and has been for too long, and described the move as removing "a dose of accommodation." That wording matters. It suggests the Fed didn't think rates were high enough to cool inflation before this hike.

The dot plot

The Fed's quarterly projections showed 16 of 18 officials expecting at least one more hike this year, up from nine in June. The median year-end projection rose to about 4.1%. Officials also lowered their unemployment forecast to 4.1% for both 2026 and 2027, a sign they see the economy as strong enough to handle higher rates.

What happened after

Stocks actually rose after the announcement. But strong business data a week later pushed Treasury yields to new highs, and by late September futures were pricing roughly a 75% chance of another hike at the October 28 meeting.

What this means for you

Credit card APRs and other variable-rate debt adjust quickly after a hike, so paying down balances is worth more now. On the flip side, high-yield savings accounts and CDs pay more. For interviews, be ready to explain why a central bank hikes into a strong jobs market with 3.4% inflation, and what "removing accommodation" signals.

A roller-coaster month left the S&P 500 roughly flat, the Dow sharply lower, and bond investors nursing big losses.

September was a tug-of-war between strong tech earnings optimism and rising interest rates. By the September 28 close, the S&P 500 sat at 7,670.84, down about 0.2% for the month. The Dow was down about 3.5%, which would snap a five-month winning streak. The Nasdaq held up better, gaining more than 1%.

The real story was bonds

The 10-year Treasury yield closed above 5% on September 14 for the first time since 2007. It kept climbing after the Fed hike and a hot business-activity survey, and reached about 5.2% in the final week of the month. The 5-year yield also crossed 5% for the first time since 2007. Rising oil, sticky inflation, and a wave of borrowing by AI companies all added pressure.

Why higher yields hurt stocks

When safe Treasuries pay 5%, investors demand more from riskier assets like stocks. Higher yields also reduce the present value of future profits, which hits fast-growing tech companies hardest. That's why tech wobbled on days when yields jumped.

Financials had a rough month

The S&P 500 financials sector fell about 6.3% in September, its worst month since March 2023. Bank of America's warning on deal fees hit bank stocks, and stress in private credit dragged asset managers down: Blackstone fell about 21% and BlackRock about 8%.

The final week

On September 28, the U.S. rejected Iran's proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Oil jumped, yields rose again, and the S&P 500 fell 0.8%, wiping out its September gain. A day later, consumer confidence came in at a 12-year low.

Why this matters

This month is a great case study in how bonds drive stocks. Notice who got hurt: long-duration assets (tech, bonds, housing) and firms that depend on deals and credit. Explaining that chain in an interview, from oil to inflation to yields to valuations, shows you actually understand markets.

Renewed attacks on Gulf shipping and a failed plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz pushed crude back above $100, and kept inflation fears alive.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  1st October 2026

Oil was the single biggest driver of markets this month. Brent crude, the global benchmark, gained more than 17% in September and U.S. crude (WTI) rose more than 10%, according to CNBC. On September 24, Brent closed at $106.60 and WTI at $94.61.

What's behind it

The conflict between the U.S., Israel, and Iran, which began in late February, has choked traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, the channel that normally carries about a fifth of the world's oil. Strikes on tankers escalated in early September, and Iran-allied Houthi militants fired missiles toward Saudi Arabia's Red Sea export terminal at Yanbu.

A deal that didn't happen

Late in the month, Iran proposed reopening the strait within seven days. On September 28, President Trump rejected the plan, and Brent climbed above $107. Some ships are still getting through with U.S. military escorts: MarineTraffic counted 132 transits from September 21–27, up from 116 the week before. Still, JPMorgan estimated Middle East oil flows at about 17 million barrels a day, only a bit more than 70% of 2025 levels.

Why it matters beyond the gas pump

Energy is the main reason U.S. inflation is stuck at 3.4%. Gas prices were up more than 27% from a year earlier in August, and jet fuel costs have pushed airfares up about 23%. As long as oil stays high, the Fed has reason to keep hiking, which is why oil, bond yields, and stocks have been moving together all month.

What to watch

Watch weekly tanker traffic through Hormuz and any news from U.S.-Iran talks. A real reopening could send oil sharply lower and ease pressure on inflation and rates. A breakdown could push Brent higher still. It's a perfect example of how one geopolitical chokepoint can move every asset class at once.

President Trump hosted Chinese President Xi Jinping at the White House on Thursday, September 24, for a state visit centered on trade, artificial intelligence, and the broader rivalry between the two countries.

The trade truce, extended

The truce that ended last year's trade war was set to expire in mid-November. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the two sides agreed to extend it by two months, into January. That's shorter than many hoped, though some analysts think the tight timeline gives both sides leverage for future talks.

A $30 billion tariff deal

After the summit, the two countries agreed to cut tariffs on about $30 billion of goods, including agricultural exports, toys, and small appliances. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said more details would follow, and a new Board of Trade will handle further negotiations.

What didn't get solved

There was no comprehensive agreement on the bigger issues, like rare earth minerals, soybean purchases, or Taiwan. The leaders will meet again soon: Trump is set to travel to China for APEC in November, and Xi is scheduled to attend the G20 in Miami in December.

What to watch

The truce now runs into January, so watch the Board of Trade talks and the November APEC meeting. Rare earths are the key pressure point for chips, EVs, and defense supply chains. Trade policy feeds directly into inflation and corporate earnings, which makes it a strong topic for interviews.

Gas, diesel, and airfares pushed prices higher in August, and the report sealed the case for a Fed rate hike.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  1st October 2026

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on September 11 that consumer prices rose 0.4% in August, up from 0.1% in July. Compared with a year earlier, prices were up 3.4%, the same pace as July and in line with forecasts. That's still well above the Fed's 2% goal, which inflation has now exceeded for more than five years.

Energy did the heavy lifting

Gasoline prices rose 3.9% in the month and accounted for more than a third of the overall increase. From a year earlier, gas was up more than 27% and energy prices overall about 16%. The conflict with Iran, which passed its six-month mark at the end of August, is the main reason.

It's spreading beyond the pump

Higher jet fuel costs pushed airfares up nearly 3% in August and more than 23% from a year ago. Shelter costs rose 0.3%, faster than July's 0.1%. Economists at Capital Economics told CNBC the risks to inflation look tilted to the upside.

Wholesale prices ran even hotter

The day before, the Producer Price Index showed wholesale inflation at 5.4% from a year earlier, a bit above forecasts. Energy prices at the wholesale level jumped 4.2% in a single month, led by a 24.1% surge in diesel. Wholesale costs often show up in consumer prices later, which is why the Fed watches them closely.

What's next

The September CPI report comes out on October 14, two weeks before the Fed's October 28 decision. If energy keeps pushing prices up, another rate hike becomes more likely.

Hiring Rebounds: 162,000 Jobs in August

A much stronger-than-expected jobs report ended a slow summer and pushed the Fed closer to raising rates.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  1st October 2026

The Bureau of Labor Statistics said on September 4 that employers added 162,000 jobs in August, about three times the consensus forecast of 53,000. The unemployment rate held at 4.1%, as expected.

Where the jobs came from

Hiring was broad. Leisure and hospitality added about 62,000 jobs, mostly at restaurants and bars, and local government education rebounded by roughly 42,000 after a weak July. Private employers added about 127,000 jobs, and the share of industries adding workers hit its highest level since December 2024.

The weak spots

Two sectors lost jobs: information shed about 23,000 and financial activities about 11,000. That's worth noting if you're job hunting in finance. The unemployment rate for college graduates stayed low at 2.7%.

Revisions and wages

June and July were revised up by a combined 55,000, lifting the three-month average to about 71,000 jobs a month. Average hourly earnings rose 0.3% to $37.75, and labor force participation edged up to 61.6%.

Why it mattered for the Fed

After the report, futures raised the odds of a September hike to about 60%, and the CPI report a week later sealed it. President Trump called it a great jobs number but argued the Fed should cut, not hike.

What's next

The September jobs report is due Friday, October 2. A strong number would keep an October rate hike on the table; a weak one could give the Fed a reason to pause.

ECB and Bank of Japan Hike Too

The Fed wasn't alone. Energy-driven inflation pushed central banks around the world to raise rates in September.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  1st October 2026

September was a month of global tightening. Three of the world's biggest central banks raised rates within about a week of each other.

European Central Bank: September 10

The ECB raised its three key rates by a quarter point, taking the deposit rate to 2.50%. It was the second hike of 2026, after a first move in June and a pause in July, when a Gulf truce briefly looked likely to ease energy prices. That truce fell apart. Eurozone inflation rose to 3.3% in August from 2.9% in July, driven by energy prices up 14.3% from a year earlier. President Christine Lagarde said future decisions will be made meeting by meeting, and ECB staff expect inflation to average 3.0% this year.

Bank of Japan: September 18

The BOJ raised its policy rate to 1.25%, the highest since 1995, just three months after its previous hike. Two of nine board members voted against it. Surprisingly, the yen weakened about 0.45% to roughly 156.6 per dollar, even though the U.S. and Japan had coordinated to support the currency. Japan's rates are still far below U.S. rates, so the move didn't change the math for investors much.

Bank of England: held

The Bank of England kept its rate at 3.75%, in a 6–3 vote.

Why this matters

When several central banks tighten at once, borrowing gets more expensive worldwide and currencies move based on who hikes more. That's why the dollar stayed strong and the yen stayed weak despite Japan's hike, a great example of rate differentials for interviews.

On Monday, September 14, Bank of America CEO Brian Moynihan warned that the bank's investment banking fees would be lower in the third quarter. BofA stock closed at $59.47, down 5.1%, its worst day since April 2025.

The rest of Wall Street followed

Goldman Sachs fell about 4%, Morgan Stanley 3.6%, and Citigroup 1.9%, according to Bloomberg data cited by Quartz. JPMorgan and Wells Fargo slipped 1–2%. Citi offered a slightly better picture the same day, saying its investment banking revenue was on pace for low-single-digit growth, with trading up mid-single digits.

Not all bad news

Moynihan said the rest of BofA's business was performing as expected. Net interest income growth was at the top of its 6–8% forecast range, and wealth management fees were up 10–15% in the quarter. He described the underlying economy as good.

Earlier in the year, deal-making was booming. Goldman's investment banking fees jumped 52% in the first half of 2026 to about $6.2 billion. A slowdown now, with rates and bond yields rising, would be a notable turn. By month end, the S&P 500 financials sector was down about 6.3%, its worst month since March 2023.

What this means for you

Investment banking deal flow is sensitive to rates and market swings. If you're recruiting, watch the big banks' third-quarter earnings in mid-October for clues about hiring. Areas like wealth management, trading, and credit look steadier right now.

Investors kept trying to pull money out of private credit funds, and the stocks of the firms that run them sank.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  1st October 2026

Private credit, loans made by investment firms instead of banks, grew into a roughly $1.8 trillion market. This year it has come under pressure, and September added to the strain.

Withdrawals capped again

On September 3, Bloomberg reported that Blackstone's $77 billion Private Credit Fund (BCRED), the largest of its kind, let investors withdraw only half of the 10% they asked for. It was the second quarter in a row. Funds like this limit withdrawals to 5% a quarter, and other managers hit the same wall: Cliffwater's flagship fund capped redemptions at 5% after investors asked for about 16%.

Loan quality is slipping

Fitch put the private credit default rate at 6.3% over the 12 months through August. Blackstone said its weakest 5% of loans were valued at about 60 cents on the dollar, compared with about 95 cents for the fund's broader senior loan portfolio. Concerns have centered on loans to software companies.

The stocks took a hit

Blackstone shares fell about 21% in September and BlackRock about 8%, according to CNBC. Apollo and Blue Owl also slid. Some analysts also point to the firms' big bets on financing AI data centers, at a time when some projects are being delayed.

Some signs of relief

Withdrawal requests at several managers eased slightly in the third quarter. At Ares, requests fell to 13.1% of shares from 14.4%, and at Apollo to 14.7% from 16.8%. Blackstone President Jon Gray said he expects investor confidence to recover.

Why this matters

Private credit is one of the fastest-growing corners of finance and a big recruiter. Understanding why "semi-liquid" funds limit withdrawals, and what happens when loans go bad, is a strong interview topic right now.

Confidence Sinks as Mortgage Rates Climb

Businesses are booming, but everyday Americans are feeling worse about the economy.

Consumer confidence fell to a 12-year low in September, according to the Conference Board data reported on September 29. For the first time since September 2024, consumers' view of current business conditions turned negative.

What people are worried about

Views of the job market also got worse, though they stayed in positive territory. Looking six months ahead, consumers expected both business conditions and the job market to weaken. With gas up more than 27% from a year ago, it's not hard to see why.

Borrowing is getting expensive

As the 10-year Treasury yield climbed above 5%, the average 30-year mortgage rate reached about 7.45%, its highest since April 2024, according to CNBC. Higher rates make homes, cars, and credit card balances more expensive to finance.

A split economy

The mood is very different in the business world. S&P Global's flash survey showed U.S. business activity growing at its fastest pace since July 2021, with a reading of 58.4. Reuters described economic sentiment as poor even as businesses spend heavily on investment. That gap between strong business data and gloomy consumers is one of the big puzzles heading into the fall.

What this means for you

If you're thinking about a car loan or a big purchase on credit, rates are the highest they've been in a while. It's a good time to build savings, which now earn more, and to pay down variable-rate debt.

Bank of America: bond yields are the new top fear

BofA's monthly Global Fund Manager Survey, taken September 4–10, showed investors cooling from extreme optimism. The sentiment gauge fell to 7.0 from 8.0, and cash holdings rose to 3.9% from 3.5%, the biggest monthly jump since March. Big bank research is a great way to learn how professionals think. Notice how each bank starts from the same data but emphasizes different risks. In interviews, being able to say "J.P. Morgan expects X, but TD thinks markets are overpricing Y" shows you follow the debate.

Nomura's RBI hike call

Foreign portfolio investors (FPIs) had returned to Indian stocks in July and August, investing about ₹20,200 crore and ₹29,630 crore. In September, they reversed course.

The selling built through the month

FPIs pulled out about ₹7,443 crore in the first week of September, about ₹13,138 crore by September 11, and about ₹20,974 crore by September 18. That pushed total foreign selling for 2026 to roughly ₹2.45 trillion, already more than all of 2025. In the first half of the month, selling hit 14 of 23 sectors, led by financial services and autos, while healthcare attracted buying.

Why: oil and U.S. yields

India imports most of its oil, so expensive crude widens its trade gap, raises inflation, and weakens the rupee. At the same time, a 5% U.S. Treasury yield makes safe American bonds more attractive than emerging markets. Analysts at Geojit and YES Securities pointed to both as the main pressures.

The rupee and stocks

On September 29, the rupee fell to about 96.13 per dollar and the Sensex dropped about 500 points in early trade, after FIIs sold about ₹5,353 crore the previous day.

What's next for India's central bank

Nomura now expects the Reserve Bank of India to raise rates at both its October and December meetings, warning that inflation could top 6%, even as it forecasts 7% economic growth for the fiscal year.

Why this matters

This is how Fed policy travels around the world. When U.S. rates rise, money flows out of riskier markets, their currencies weaken, and their central banks may have to hike too. It's a key concept for anyone interested in global markets or international finance.

Rates 101: The Fed Hike and the 5% 10-Year

The Fed started raising rates again, and the bond market went further. Here's how the pieces fit together.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  1st October 2026
3.75–4.00%
Fed funds range
~5.2%
10-year Treasury
~7.45%
30-year mortgage rate
~75%
Odds of an Oct 28 hike

On September 16 the Fed raised its target range by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%. But the bigger move came in the bond market: the 10-year Treasury yield topped 5% on September 14 for the first time since 2007, then rose to about 5.2% in the final week of the month.

Fed funds rate vs. the 10-year yield

The fed funds rate is the overnight rate banks charge each other, and the Fed sets it directly. The 10-year Treasury yield is set by the market. It reflects where investors expect rates, inflation, and growth to go over the next decade. This month, strong data, high oil, and heavy borrowing by AI companies all pushed it up.

Why bond prices fall when yields rise

A bond pays a fixed coupon. When new bonds start paying more, older lower-paying bonds become less attractive, so their prices drop until their yields match the market. Rising yields always mean falling prices for existing bonds. It's one of the most common interview questions out there.

What's next

The median Fed projection points to about 4.1% by year end, implying one more hike. Futures were pricing roughly a 75% chance of that hike coming at the October 28 meeting.

What this means for you

Mortgage rates near 7.45% make buying a home harder, and variable-rate debt costs more. But Treasury bills, CDs, and high-yield savings accounts now pay some of the best rates in years. If you're recruiting, be ready to explain the price-yield relationship and why long-term yields can rise even faster than the Fed moves.

Currencies: A Month of Central Bank Hikes

The Fed, ECB, and Bank of Japan all raised rates in September. Here's why the yen still ended up weak.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  1st October 2026

Yen per dollar after BOJ

September brought a wave of central bank decisions. The ECB raised its deposit rate to 2.50% on September 10. The Fed hiked on September 16. The Bank of Japan raised its rate to 1.25% on September 18, the highest since 1995. The Bank of England held at 3.75%.

How to read a currency quote

USD/JPY at 156.6 means one U.S. dollar buys about 156.6 yen. If that number goes up, the dollar is getting stronger against the yen.

Why rate hikes usually strengthen a currency

Money tends to flow where it earns more. When a central bank raises rates, holding that currency pays better, so demand rises. That's the textbook view.

The yen puzzle

Even after its hike, the Bank of Japan's rate is far below the Fed's, so the incentive to hold dollars over yen barely changed. The yen weakened about 0.45% after the decision, to around 156.6 per dollar, even though the U.S. and Japan had already intervened together to support it. Two board members voted against the hike, which also softened its impact.

Why this matters

The gap between two countries' interest rates is one of the biggest drivers of exchange rates. A weak yen makes Japanese exports cheaper and affects any company, or investor, doing business across borders. Understanding rate differentials is a big plus for markets and corporate finance roles.

Oil surged, gold struggled against rising yields, and bitcoin traded like a risk asset.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  1st October 2026

Brent crude gained more than 17% in September and closed at $106.60 on September 24, as the conflict with Iran kept traffic through the Strait of Hormuz far below normal. U.S. crude (WTI) closed that day at $94.61.

Brent vs. WTI

Brent is the global benchmark, priced off North Sea crude. WTI is the U.S. benchmark, priced in Oklahoma. Brent reacts more to Middle East supply shocks because so much seaborne oil normally moves through Hormuz, which is why the gap between the two is so wide right now.

Why gold struggled

Gold pays no interest. When Treasury yields rise, holding bonds or cash becomes more rewarding than holding gold. Gold fell to about $4,370 an ounce on September 1 as yields and the dollar climbed, and rising yields kept pressure on it through the month.

Crypto as a risk asset

Bitcoin fell below $84,000 on September 24, touching about $83,200, as the 10-year yield hit its highest level since 2007. Lately it has traded more like a high-risk tech stock than a hedge, falling when rates jump.

What to watch

If a Hormuz deal comes together, oil could drop quickly, and yields, gold, and crypto would likely react too. Watch gas prices as well; they usually follow crude within a few weeks.
