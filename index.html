Cover Story · October 2026

The Fed Hits Pause, but the Inflation Fight Isn't Over

A weak jobs report talked the Fed out of a second hike in October. A hot inflation report and $115 oil mean December is still very much in play.

Last month the big question was whether the Fed would hike again in October. Early in the month, the answer started to look like no. The September jobs report, released October 2, showed employers added only 29,000 jobs, far below forecasts, and unemployment ticked up to 4.2%. Markets quickly cut the odds of an October hike to around 14%, from roughly 70% earlier that week.

Then inflation pushed back. The September CPI report on October 14 showed prices up 3.7% from a year earlier, the fastest pace in months, with gasoline alone up 9.1% in a single month. Oil, which briefly slipped below $100 at the start of October, climbed back to about $115 as attacks on Gulf energy sites and tanker traffic picked up.

On October 28, the Fed split the difference. It held rates at 3.75–4.00% in a 10–2 vote, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissenting in favor of another quarter-point hike. The statement upgraded the economy to "expanding at a strong pace" and said inflation remains elevated. Futures now put the odds of a December hike above 80%.

For students, the lesson is that the Fed is now balancing two signals pointing in opposite directions: a cooling job market and inflation that refuses to settle. Being able to explain why the Fed waited, and what would make it move in December, is exactly the kind of thinking interviewers want to hear.

Fed Holds at 3.75–4.00%, but Two Officials Wanted a Hike

The Fed paused after September's increase. The two dissents, and a sunnier view of the economy, keep December in play.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  29th October 2026

On October 28, the Federal Open Market Committee voted 10–2 to keep the federal funds rate at 3.75–4.00%, where it has been since September's quarter-point hike. The decision was widely expected: a Reuters survey found 94 of 100 economists predicted a hold.

The two dissenters

Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both voted to raise rates by another quarter point. In September, the vote to hike had been unanimous, so a split this time tells you some officials think the Fed is moving too slowly.

What changed in the statement

The Fed described the economy as expanding at a "strong pace," an upgrade from "solid" in September. It noted resilient consumer spending, strong productivity, and robust business investment, but said inflation remains elevated. It also said it wants more information before acting again, which is why it waited.

Warsh's other news

Chair Kevin Warsh announced that starting in 2027, one of the Fed's eight yearly meetings will focus only on long-term strategy, with no rate vote. He also made clear he doesn't like giving markets hints about future moves, saying "I don't believe in forward guidance" outside of a crisis. Asked about next week's midterm elections, he said politics played no role in the decision.

What's priced in

After the decision, the CME FedWatch tool showed about an 83% chance of a hike at the December 8–9 meeting. That meeting will also include a new dot plot showing where officials expect rates to go.

What this means for you

Rates didn't move this month, so your credit card APR and savings yields should stay about where they are. But most of the market expects one more hike in December, so the window to lock in a high CD rate or pay down variable-rate debt is still open. In interviews, be ready to explain why a central bank might wait even when inflation is rising.

Inflation Climbs to 3.7% as Gas Prices Spike

The September CPI report showed prices rising faster, and not just at the pump.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  29th October 2026

The Bureau of Labor Statistics said on October 14 that consumer prices rose 0.6% in September, up from 0.4% in August. From a year earlier, inflation rose to 3.7%, up from 3.4% and a bit above the 3.6% economists expected.

Gas did most of the work

The gasoline index jumped 9.1% in a single month and accounted for more than half of the overall increase. That traces straight back to the conflict in the Middle East and the disruptions to oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz.

But it's not only gas

Core CPI, which leaves out food and energy, rose 0.3% for the month and 2.6% from a year earlier, both slightly hotter than forecast. TD Economics noted that core prices failed to cool as hoped, and said the report strengthens the case for another near-term rate hike. Food prices, meanwhile, were flat for the month.

Why the Fed still waited

The Fed's favorite inflation gauge is the PCE index, not CPI. Its September core reading, released October 29, rose 0.3% for the month, matching expectations. Combined with the weak jobs report, that gave the Fed room to hold in October.

What's next

The October CPI report comes out November 10, a month before the Fed's December decision. If gas prices keep climbing with oil, another hike becomes even more likely.

Revisions, July–August

The Bureau of Labor Statistics said on October 2 that employers added just 29,000 jobs in September. Economists had expected around 90,000, and the number was below the prior year's average of about 45,000 a month.

Revisions made it worse

July and August were revised down by a combined 60,000 jobs. August, which first came in at a strong 162,000 and helped push the Fed to hike in September, was cut to about 133,000.

Wages and unemployment

The unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2% from 4.1%. Average hourly earnings rose only 0.1% to $37.81, and are up 3.0% from a year earlier. That's slower than 3.7% inflation, which means the typical worker's paycheck is losing buying power.

The market reaction

The odds of an October rate hike fell to about 14%, from roughly 70% earlier that week. Oil also dipped below $100 that day. Not every economist read the report as bad news, though: First Trust pointed out that a different survey of households showed a big jump in employment.

What's next

The October jobs report is due Friday, November 6. Another weak number would make December harder for the Fed; a rebound would make a hike more likely.

Brent Swings From Under $100 Back to About $115

Oil started October below $100. By the end of the month, attacks on Gulf energy sites had pushed it up about 17%.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  29th October 2026

Last issue, oil was the month's biggest story as Brent jumped about 17% in September. October looked like it might bring relief: Brent briefly fell below $100 on October 2. It didn't last. By late October, Brent was trading around $114 to $117 a barrel, up roughly 17% over the past month.

What pushed prices back up

A wave of attacks on tankers near the Strait of Hormuz pushed Brent above $117 in the last week of October. Iran-allied Houthi militants also said they hit Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery with a missile. An ANZ Research note said traffic through Hormuz is running at about 60% of normal, and Gulf crude exports have fallen for four straight weeks.

The risk premium

One investment chief told CNBC that geopolitical uncertainty has added roughly $20 a barrel to oil prices. Global inventories have also fallen sharply after eight months of conflict, so there's less of a cushion if supply gets disrupted again. CNBC also reported that the U.S. may be preparing to renew strikes on Iran after the midterm elections.

A slight cooldown at month end

Prices eased on October 29 after reports that Vice President JD Vance sent a letter to Iranian officials to try to revive peace talks, and after Saudi diplomats held meetings in Tehran.

What this means for you

Gas prices follow crude oil within a few weeks, so expect to keep paying more at the pump. Watch the gap between Brent and WTI: it's unusually wide because Brent reacts more to Middle East supply problems. It's a great talking point if you're interested in commodities.

Alphabet and Microsoft both reported quarterly results after the close on October 28. The next morning, they went in opposite directions.

Alphabet's miss

Alphabet earned $2.12 a share, well below the $2.94 analysts expected, and its revenue came up short too. It also reported negative free cash flow of about $9.07 billion, meaning its spending on data centers and equipment was bigger than the cash its businesses brought in. The stock fell about 13% on October 29, its steepest drop since it went public in 2004, wiping out roughly $500 billion in market value.

Microsoft's beat

Microsoft beat expectations, with adjusted earnings of $4.89 a share versus $4.72 expected. Its Azure cloud business grew 48%. Microsoft is also spending heavily on AI, but its cash from operations still covered that spending with plenty to spare.

Software stocks got hit too

ServiceNow fell about 18% after missing earnings expectations, and Fortinet dropped about 26% on slowing product sales. Amazon reports after the close on October 29, and Apple reports on November 2.

Why this matters

The market's question has shifted from "who's spending on AI?" to "whose AI spending is paying off?" Free cash flow is the number that answered it this week. If you're preparing for interviews, practice explaining the difference between earnings and free cash flow, and why investors care about both.

On Monday, October 26, Anthropic filed its S-1 registration statement with the SEC. It plans to list its Class A shares on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker ANT. The company had filed confidentially in June, so this was the first time its detailed financials became public.

What the filing shows

Anthropic reported $16.3 billion in revenue for the first half of 2026, but it is not profitable. Most of its large reported net loss comes from non-cash accounting adjustments tied to convertible notes, and its operating loss for the period was about $693 million. As of June 30, it had raised about $141.1 billion in total capital, and as of September 30 it had more than 11,900 customers paying it at least $100,000 a year.

How big could it be?

The filing doesn't set a price or share count yet. IPO watchers expect it could raise around $75 billion, comparable to SpaceX's record IPO, and earlier reports put the figure as high as $100 billion. Anthropic's last private funding round, in May 2026, valued it at about $965 billion.

What happens next

Next comes a roadshow, where the company and its bankers pitch the shares to big investors, followed by pricing and the first day of trading. No listing date has been set.

Why this matters

A mega-IPO touches almost every part of Wall Street: underwriting fees for the banks running the deal, valuation work for analysts, and demand from fund managers. If you're interested in equity capital markets, follow how this deal gets priced. A good interview question to practice: how would you value a fast-growing company that isn't profitable yet?

Last issue covered Bank of America's September 14 warning about weak third-quarter deal fees, which dragged bank stocks down. When the big banks reported on October 13 and 14, the picture was better than that warning suggested.

Goldman Sachs: the standout

Goldman earned $17.01 a share on $18.68 billion in revenue, its second-best quarter ever. Investment banking fees rose 21% from a year earlier to $3.22 billion, beating estimates by about $384 million, and advisory fees hit a quarterly record of $1.65 billion. JPMorgan: a rare miss

JPMorgan earned $5.57 a share, below expectations, partly because of a $658 million loss on investment securities. BofA's investment banking fees fell 11%, matching its earlier warning. Deal-making slowed but didn't collapse, and trading desks are busy. If you're recruiting, that's encouraging news for both banking and markets roles. Also notice the gap between a headline and the data: September's warning sounded scary, but the full results told a more balanced story.

The Nasdaq hit an all-time high early in the month. Then AI doubts and higher oil made the ride bumpier.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  29th October 2026

October started strong for tech. On October 6, the Nasdaq Composite closed at a record 27,599.89, helped by the AI and chip rally.

The late-month wobble

Stocks slipped in the second half of the month. The S&P 500 fell about 1.2% on October 22, to 7,663, and dipped again the next day to about 7,624, as oil climbed and investors grew nervous about AI spending.

Three straight gains into the Fed

Stocks then rallied for three days in a row. On October 28, the day of the Fed decision, the S&P 500 closed at 7,743.98, the Nasdaq at 26,549.85, and the Dow at 52,475.10. That left the S&P 500 about 1% above its September 28 close, and still below its 52-week high of about 7,845.

Narrow leadership

One warning sign: on October 28, the equal-weight version of the S&P 500 actually slipped slightly while the regular index rose. That means a handful of giant companies did most of the lifting, which is exactly why Alphabet's drop the next day hit the indexes so hard.

Why this matters

Index levels can hide what's happening underneath. When a few mega-cap stocks drive most of the gains, the market becomes more fragile to bad news from any one of them. Comparing the regular and equal-weight S&P 500 is a simple way to check how broad a rally really is.

Consumer confidence bounced back from September's 12-year low, but people still plan to spend carefully.

The Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index rose 7.5 points in October to 91.7, its highest reading since June and far above the 81.0 economists expected. It was the first increase in four months, and September was revised up to 84.2.

What improved

The Conference Board said the gain was driven mainly by better views of business conditions, both now and in the months ahead. The Present Situation Index, which measures how people feel about today's business and job conditions, rose to 120.4 from 111.8.

The catch

Even with better moods, consumers said they plan to spend less on services and on big-ticket items like appliances and cars. With gas prices up and wages growing more slowly than inflation, people are feeling better but not splurging.

Why this matters

Consumer spending drives roughly two-thirds of the U.S. economy, so economists watch confidence closely. But surveys don't always match what people actually do. Comparing what consumers say with what they spend is a useful habit when reading economic data.

October was a pause month for the world's big central banks after September's wave of hikes.

European Central Bank: October 29

The ECB kept its deposit rate at 2.50%, as expected, after raising it twice this year. It warned that inflation risks are tilted to the upside because of the recent surge in oil and gas prices. Lagarde said the board debated a hike, and money markets priced about a 67% chance of a December move. A UBS economist called it a hawkish pause rather than the end of the hiking cycle.

Lagarde's surprise

Hours before the decision, Lagarde announced she will step down in February 2027, about nine months before her term ends. The ECB is also watching France closely: French government bond yields have climbed to their highest since 2002. Lagarde said the ECB has tools to respond if bond markets become disorderly, but suggested no intervention is imminent.

Bank of Japan: October 30

The Bank of Japan announces its decision on Friday, October 30, and is widely expected to hold at 1.25%. Traders see December as the more likely month for another hike, as a weak yen and higher energy costs add to inflation in Japan.

Why this matters

Three big central banks are all saying the same thing: inflation from energy isn't going away, and more hikes may be needed. When several countries tighten at once, global borrowing costs rise together, which matters for everything from mortgages to corporate bonds.

Rates: The Fed Waits, the Bond Market Doesn't Relax

Even with the Fed on hold, the 10-year Treasury yield stayed near 5%.

By Aman Lathiya, Secretary  ·  Finance Club NYC  ·  29th October 2026

The Fed held rates in October, but bond yields didn't come down much. The 10-year Treasury yield was near 5.00% after the decision, close to the highest levels since 2007 reached in September.

Why yields stayed high

Inflation is running at 3.7%, oil is back near $115, and markets expect another Fed hike in December. All three keep pressure on yields. After the Fed decision, Treasury yields steepened, meaning long-term yields rose relative to short-term ones.

The PCE check

The Fed's preferred inflation gauge, core PCE, rose 0.3% in September, matching expectations. No surprise there, which helped the Fed justify waiting.

A global story

Bond yields are rising in Europe too. French government yields hit their highest since 2002 amid political and budget turmoil, and the ECB said it's ready to step in if markets become disorderly.

Why this matters

When the 10-year yield sits near 5%, mortgages, car loans, and corporate borrowing all stay expensive. For investors, safe bonds paying 5% make stocks look less attractive by comparison. Watch whether yields fall if the Fed signals it's done hiking.

Since October 1, the dollar has traded between just above 156 and just under 159 yen. It touched about 158.95 on October 14 after the hot U.S. inflation report, then dropped toward 156 on October 23 when Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda sounded hawkish.

Why 160 matters

Japan's Finance Ministry has spent about ¥27.13 trillion this year buying yen whenever the dollar rose above 160. Officials have never officially named 160 as a target, but traders treat it as the line where intervention risk jumps.

The rate gap

The Fed's rate is 3.75–4.00% while the Bank of Japan's is 1.25%. That gap makes holding dollars more rewarding than holding yen, which keeps pressure on the yen even after Japan raised rates twice this year.

Euro and the dollar

The dollar index sat near 102.2 at month end, and the dollar gained against the euro after the ECB's October 29 hold.

Why this matters

Currency intervention is when a government buys or sells its own currency to change its price. It can move the yen 3–4% in a single day. If you're interested in FX or global macro, this is the setup to watch around the Bank of Japan's October 30 decision.

Gold cooled off

Gold traded around $4,020 an ounce, down about 3.6% over the past month and well below its January record of about $5,608. Gold pays no interest, so when bond yields sit near 5%, it gets harder for gold to compete.

Bitcoin lost momentum

Bitcoin was near $80,000 on October 29, down from mid-month highs near $87,000 and about 27% below where it was a year ago. It briefly dipped under $80,000 after Alphabet's results and a chip-profit miss at Samsung, which shows how closely bitcoin now trades with tech stocks.

Corporate buyers

Publicly listed companies now hold about 1.3 million bitcoin, up 31% over the past 14 months, with Strategy responsible for most of that increase.

What to watch

Oil is still the swing factor for inflation. A real peace deal could send it down quickly, while new attacks could push it higher. Gold and bitcoin will likely keep reacting to bond yields and tech stocks.

Finance Tip of the Month
#2
October 2026 — Know Your Own Inflation Rate
"The news says inflation is 3.7%, but your own number could be higher or lower. Here's how to check: open your bank or card statement from this month and the same month last year, and compare what you spent on your three biggest categories, like gas, food, and your phone bill. If one jumped a lot, that's where to look for savings first, maybe by carpooling, cooking a few more meals, or switching plans. And keep a small emergency fund in a high-yield savings account. With rates still high, it earns you real interest while it sits there."
